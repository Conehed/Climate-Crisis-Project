How Rising Temperatures affet California Wildfires

Increase in annual burned area

California’s annual burned area increased fivefold between 1972 and 2018, representing a 405% increase. Researchers identified rising temperatures as the main driver of this trend, showing that major fire years were part of a broader pattern.

Increase in summer forest fires

The area burned by summer forest fires increased eightfold during the study period. The greatest increases occurred in the North Coast and Sierra Nevada forests, where warmer summer conditions have contributed to more extensive burning.

Hotter and drier air

Researchers found the strongest relationship between summer forest fires and atmospheric aridity, which combines heat with a lack of water vapor. These hot, dry conditions help dry vegetation, making more fuel available to burn.

Temperature and atmospheric dryness

Between 1896 and 2018, maximum temperatures from March through October increased by 1.81°C. This corresponded to a 13% increase in vapor pressure deficit, a measure of atmospheric dryness. The observed changes matched climate model expectations for warming caused by human emissions.

Greater fall fire risk

Rising temperatures have increased fall fire risk even though researchers found no significant increase in strong wind events or changes in winter rain timing. Vegetation is already dry in the fall because it has received little rain since spring, and additional warming makes it even drier. When strong winds and late rains occur together, these conditions can produce destructive fires.

Human activities amplify the effects

Fire suppression has increased the amount of vegetation available to burn in some areas. Meanwhile, people living in forests create more potential ignition sources. Together, these factors strengthen the effects of warming when hot, dry conditions arrive.
